# Market effects we can reproduce, and why the sim now has expiring derivatives

*Research note, 2026-07-18. Sources collected in `~/autoresearch-runs/exchange-sim-ideas/DEEPRESEARCH.md`; experimental evidence from the gen-0 to gen-7 campaign in `EXPERIMENTS.md` on branch `autoresearch/exchange-sim-gen1`.*

## What we set out to answer

We researched which of the Bouchaud-school market effects an agent-based exchange simulation can actually reproduce, what minimal agent mechanism generates each one, and what was missing from the instrument set. The short answer on the last point: nothing in a spot-plus-perpetuals world lets a participant trade volatility as a thing in itself, and several of the most interesting connectivity effects (hedging feedback, expiry pinning, basis convergence) only exist when contracts have a finite life. So this pass added cash-settled dated futures and European options as first-class instrument types, with the same accounting rigor as the perpetuals. They are implemented, tested, and conserving money to the unit through full listing-to-settlement cycles.

## Effects within reach, and what our own data already shows

The stylized facts of Cont's classic checklist (heavy tails, volatility clustering, no linear autocorrelation) are the baseline any serious simulator must pass (see the survey here https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/Empirical-properties-of-asset-returns:-stylized-and-Cont/b674f1384a95948d52018d1748e7284ef566233c, and a 2025 revisit with updated thresholds here https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/14697688.2025.2530637). We do not have to speculate about whether our engine can produce them, because the overnight campaign already measured it: a self-exciting taker with an exponential Hawkes kernel took excess kurtosis of one-second returns from 5.5 to 8.8 and the trade-count Fano factor from 0.8 to between 1.5 and 6.8, while linear return autocorrelation stayed near zero. The literature says near-critical branching is the defensible mechanism for clustering (a recent calibrated treatment on Binance and LOBSTER data is here https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.08085), and our data agrees in a satisfying way: the clustering appeared as soon as the flow became self-exciting, at no cost to price stability.

Herding is the second effect we can generate on demand, and it is the one Bouchaud himself attacked with Cont in their percolation model of coordinated trader clusters, where the cluster-size distribution at criticality follows a power law with mean-field exponent 5/2 and produces fat-tailed returns (the original is here https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/9712318). Our imbalance-coupled taker is a crude version of the same idea, and it taught us something the papers usually state as theory: coupling flow direction to the visible book is a death-spiral amplifier. In the two-hour runs it turned a slow price drift into a full collapse of all five markets onto the tick floor, with the market maker unable to quote below one tick acting as an absorbing barrier. The engine's accounting held through the entire collapse, which is exactly what you want from a laboratory: the instability is economic, not numerical. This collapse-and-anchor phenomenology, where stability depends on the capital of fundamental traders relative to flow pressure, is in practice a small ecology-stability result of its own, and it is the seed of the longer-term paper direction discussed below.

The square-root impact law is the most attractive target we have not yet run. The empirical claim is remarkably universal: a metaorder of size Q moves price like Y·σ·√(Q/V) regardless of how it is sliced, and the accepted explanation is a latent order book whose liquidity vanishes linearly near the traded price (the Tóth, Lehalle and Bouchaud latent-liquidity paper is here https://arxiv.org/abs/1105.1694, and Mastromatteo's agent-based reaction-diffusion version, which is the direct recipe for a simulator, is here https://arxiv.org/abs/1311.6262; the crossover from linear to square-root at small Q is quantified here https://arxiv.org/abs/1811.05230). The experiment writes itself with existing pieces: an execution agent that splits a parent order into child orders on a schedule, a sweep over Q, and a fit of the impact exponent with 0.5 as the target. Closely related is the propagator picture of how markets slowly digest supply and demand: order-sign autocorrelation decays as roughly τ to the power of minus one half because of order splitting, and price stays diffusive only because the bare impact propagator decays at exactly the compensating rate (Bouchaud, Farmer and Lillo's handbook chapter is here https://arxiv.org/abs/0809.0822, the fluctuation-response formulation here https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0307332). The same execution agent generates both signatures, and the analyzer already computes autocorrelations. The queue-reactive model of Huang, Lehalle and Rosenbaum is the third mechanism worth transplanting: arrival intensities that depend on queue sizes rather than constant Poisson rates (the original is here https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/01621459.2014.982278). Our taker config gets there with one more coupling term. Two further catalogue entries cost little once multiple correlated assets trade at once: the Epps effect, where measured cross-correlation rises with the sampling interval purely because of asynchronous trade arrivals (mechanism and functional form here https://arxiv.org/abs/0704.1099), and the retarded leverage effect, where past negative returns raise future volatility with an exponentially decaying memory (Bouchaud, Matacz and Potters, here https://arxiv.org/abs/cond-mat/0101120).

Two effects need architecture we deliberately deferred. Latency-race dynamics in the style of Aquilina, Budish and O'Neill (the message-data study that measured races as roughly a third of the effective spread, here https://academic.oup.com/qje/article/137/1/493/6368348) turned out to be impossible to express with polling actors: our gen-6 experiment gave four arbitrageurs a twentyfold speed spread and they captured identical profits, because a 100ms decision timer swamps any network latency difference. Races need event-driven actors that react to trade pushes. The same rebuild unlocks the frequent-batch-auction comparison of Budish, Cramton and Shim (here https://academic.oup.com/qje/article/130/4/1547/1916146), which is the natural companion experiment: run the same speed-tiered ecology under continuous matching and under a 100ms uniform-price auction and compare arb profits and spreads.

## Why expiring derivatives, and how they fit

Perpetuals never pin anything: funding nudges the basis but there is no date at which the contract must equal the underlying. Dated futures add exactly that boundary condition, which is what makes cash-and-carry convergence a testable prediction rather than a hope. Options add the ability to hold a position whose value is convexity itself, which is the only way volatility becomes a tradeable quantity and the only way delta-hedging feedback can appear in the ecology. That feedback is not a speculative worry but a theorem-grade result: Frey and Stremme showed in 1997 that dynamic hedging demand raises volatility and makes it price-dependent (here https://statmath.wu.ac.at/~frey/publications/bonnsfb310.pdf), the sign rule is that dealers short gamma buy rallies and sell dips and so amplify moves while long-gamma dealers damp them, and a 2025 treatment solves the option market maker's quoting problem under its own hedging impact, which is close to a design document for the agent we would write (here https://arxiv.org/pdf/2511.02518). Empirically, gamma squeezes are common enough to study in bulk: one recent sample counts 669 events over 2019 to 2023 with an average abnormal return above five percent on the event day. The canonical inventory-aware option market maker, quoting around a Black-Scholes theoretical value and skewing by the net vega and gamma of inventory, is Stoikov and Saglam (here https://people.orie.cornell.edu/sfs33/StoikovSaglam.pdf); expiry pinning, where the underlying clusters at strikes on expiry day through the same hedge-rebalancing channel, was documented by Ni, Pearson and Poteshman and is a free experiment once option open interest is large relative to underlying depth. These loops are the connectivity mechanisms the eventual paper cares about.

The user's framing, that different markets are just different types of API, matched the codebase well. A dated future is the perpetual margin core minus funding plus an expiry: `ExpiringFutures` embeds `PerpFutures`, reports itself as not a perp (which excludes it from the funding sweep), and exposes the embedded core to the price loop so mark updates, margin calls and liquidation sweeps come along without any engine changes. A European option could not reuse the linear margin path because its reservation is side-asymmetric, so the engine gained one small interface: an `OrderMarginer` reserves what the side requires, the full premium for buyers and a Deribit-style formula for sellers, taking max of a base rate minus out-of-the-moneyness and a floor, times the underlying, plus the mark premium. Marks come from Black-76 on the underlying mid with a flat per-instrument volatility, which is the standard simulator compromise; a smile is a config change away because volatility lives on the instrument, not in the engine.

The margin numbers turned out to match production exactly: Deribit's short-option initial margin is max(0.15 minus the out-of-the-money fraction, 0.1) times the underlying plus the option mark, with 0.075 plus mark as maintenance, which is precisely our default parameterization (their margin page is here https://support.deribit.com/hc/en-us/articles/25944811317149-Margin-types-and-usage). Settlement follows venue practice rather than convenience: the settlement price is a TWAP of the underlying observed over a configurable window before expiry (Deribit uses thirty minutes sampled every four seconds, Binance thirty minutes sampled every second; ours defaults to sixty seconds because simulation tenors are minutes, not quarters), so an empty or manipulated derivative book cannot corrupt settlement. Delivery fees mirror the venue pattern too: futures pay basis points of settlement notional, options pay basis points of underlying notional capped at a fraction of exercise value, so worthless contracts settle free. At expiry the engine cancels resting orders with exact ledger releases (actors get the same forced-cancel notification the liquidation path uses), exercises intrinsic value in cash, releases short margin through the margin ledger, charges an optional delivery fee to fee revenue, and delists. The listing side is symmetric: the exchange polls user-supplied `ListingPolicy` implementations, and two ship with the library, a tenor-grid futures lister and an option-chain lister that keeps strikes centered on spot. Lifecycle is observable, not implicit: listings and settlements are announced as `MDInstrument` messages on a reserved reference-data feed, and a `ReqQueryInstruments` request recovers the current board, so an actor written today will keep discovering contracts that do not exist yet.

Every claim above is backed by tests rather than intention: option round trips leave zero margin dust, in-the-money and out-of-the-money exercises pay exactly intrinsic, and a three-cycle simulation under full automation with random flow conserves USD across every wallet plus fee revenue to the exact unit, clean under the race detector. The known gap is mark-to-market liquidation of short options between trade and expiry; initial margin is locked but a deep adverse move only surfaces at settlement. That sweep is the next engine lever.

## The longer game

The paper direction (connectivity of participant assumptions and behavior, and whether the resulting ecology has stable equilibria) now has its laboratory pieces, and the literature's prior is worth stating plainly: there probably is no stable Nash point to find. Farmer's market-ecology program models strategy classes as populations whose returns are density-dependent on the wealth invested in them, and the Scholl, Calinescu and Farmer PNAS study finds Lotka-Volterra style predator-prey cycles among value, trend and noise traders, with the market orbiting efficiency rather than settling on it (the study is here https://arxiv.org/abs/2009.09454, Farmer's foundational treatment here https://arxiv.org/abs/adap-org/9812005). Instability as the object of study, not a bug. We already observed one instability threshold empirically: price integrity depends on anchor capital exceeding flow pressure, and herding coupling moves that threshold violently. Market-maker competition gives a second natural equilibrium question, since our three-maker experiment tightened spreads 17% and tripled depth, and somewhere above that N maker profits must cross zero. Options add the third: delta-hedging feedback as a coupling channel between the option book and the underlying, with the gamma sign of the dealer inventory deciding whether the coupling damps or amplifies. In practice the sequence that gets to a paper is event-driven actors first, then the square-root impact and batch-auction experiments as calibration anchors, then the ecology sweeps.

If massive parameter sweeps become the bottleneck, GPU-vectorized designs in the JAX-LOB style are the escape hatch (thousands of books in parallel, here https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.13289), and if data-driven flow realism is ever preferred over mechanism interpretability, generative order-flow models like TRADES exist (here https://arxiv.org/html/2502.07071v2). For this project we would stay with the mechanistic Go simulation: the paper's value is causal mechanisms, and a diffusion model that reproduces stylized facts cannot tell you which assumption produced them.

A broad architectural comparison point for everything above is ABIDES, the JPMorgan discrete-event market simulator whose agent ecology and kernel design remain the reference (here https://arxiv.org/abs/1904.12066), together with its finding that strategy performance degrades when moved from replay to interactive simulation (here https://arxiv.org/pdf/1906.12010), which is the cleanest argument for why this kind of simulator is worth building at all.
